# 4. Endurance

Endurance is four adaptations, not one. Each has its own dose.

| Adaptation | Effort | Dose | Rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| Muscular endurance | Sets of about 11-30 reps, close to failure | 2-3 sessions per muscle a week | [NA-END-004], [NA-END-005] |
| Anaerobic capacity | All-out bouts of about 20-90 seconds | About 2 days a week | [NA-END-002] |
| Maximal aerobic output | One hard effort of about 5-15 minutes | About once a week | [NA-END-003] |
| Long-duration endurance | Continuous, nasal-breathing pace, 20+ minutes | 60-120 minutes a week | [NA-END-001] |

## Muscular endurance

This quality is local: it lives in the muscles doing the work. Exercise choice therefore has to be precise. To do more push-ups, train push-ups. To hold a longer plank, train the plank. Sets run about 11 to 30 reps and end close to failure [NA-END-004]. The load only needs to match, or slightly exceed, what the athlete wants to be good at. Heavy loads cut the rep count and train something else.

Progress by adding a rep or two a week, or a few seconds a week for holds, on that exact movement [NA-END-005]. Train the larger muscle groups first, because big leg work causes some whole-body fatigue.

## Anaerobic capacity

These are the efforts that leave the athlete gasping: about 20 to 90 seconds of all-out work. A solid starting set is 30 seconds hard and 30 seconds easy, for at least 4 rounds [NA-END-002]. Shorter, 20-second bursts need more rest than work. Twice a week works better than once. Three to four or more days a week adds fatigue, hurts sleep and brings no extra benefit.

A useful self-test is a "sugarcane" set: cover as much distance as possible in 2 minutes, rest 2 minutes, repeat that distance and note the time, rest, then go for that time and try to beat the first distance. Week to week, the third round shows progress (E3, 03:11:45).

## Maximal aerobic output

This is one hard, sustained effort of about 5 to 15 minutes, close to the highest pace the athlete can hold for that time. A timed mile is a classic example. A simple weekly version: go as far as possible in 10 minutes, and record the distance [NA-END-003]. Do not put this on the same day as anaerobic capacity intervals; both suffer.

## Long-duration endurance

Plan 60 to 120 minutes a week, in sessions of 20 minutes or more, for example one long session or two of about 45 minutes [NA-END-001]. The pace allows breathing through the nose, or easy conversation. A brisk hike, an easy ride or row, a swim, or a jog all count. A shorter session of 30 to 60 minutes at a slightly harder pace, where talking becomes difficult, also works.

Use breathing as the main intensity gauge. Nasal-only breathing at a steady rhythm marks long-duration work. When the mouth has to help, the work has moved into the harder zones. Heart rate is a useful second check, not the only one. End each session by walking easily for a few minutes while the breath settles.

## Combining the four

A balanced week touches all four. Do not do only long, slow work: mixing short intervals, a hard sustained effort and easy volume gives a better result than any one of them alone. Strength work helps endurance performance, and moderate endurance work does not harm strength much. Large amounts of long-duration work can hold back strength and muscle gains, especially when food intake is low (E2, 04:01:15; see [NA-ADP-002]).

Short "exercise snacks" are an optional extra: about 20 seconds of all-out effort, such as running up a flight of stairs, a few times a day. In studies of people with desk jobs they raised VO2max. They also help after a large, high-sugar meal (E3, 00:08:36).
