# 5. Program design

A program is a set of decisions made in a fixed order. This pack follows the order Dr. Galpin teaches and adds the harness's own limits where the source leaves a number open.

## Before any exercise is chosen

1. **Pick one primary goal per block.** Name the adaptation that matters most for the next weeks. Others get maintenance doses. A narrow goal is reached faster than a broad one [NA-DES-001]. With no clear goal, let the assessment choose (see [NA-ASM-004]).
2. **Name the obstacle.** Before writing the plan, name what is most likely to break it: travel, a work peak, childcare, a joint that flares up. Plan around it on purpose [NA-DES-002].
3. **Fit the calendar.** Start from the real calendar, with fixed commitments already in it. Count training time as door to door, warm-up and cool-down included. Be conservative: three completed sessions beat five planned ones [NA-DES-003].
4. **Keep sessions bounded.** A planned session should take 60 minutes or less, warm-up and cool-down included. This is a pack policy, not a source rule. A longer session produces a warning, not a block [NA-DES-004].
5. **Plan for chaos.** Decide in advance what the minimum session looks like on a bad day or in a broken week, for example 20 minutes of the most important lifts. A short session then replaces a skipped one instead of the week collapsing [NA-DES-005].

## Inside the week

- **Session order:** fresh, neural work first and fatiguing work last (see [NA-ADP-003]). The most important exercise comes first.
- **Spacing:** keep about 48 hours between hard hypertrophy sessions for the same muscle (see [NA-HYP-005]). Keep maximal aerobic and anaerobic interval days apart (see [NA-END-003]).
- **Bedtime:** finish hard training several hours before sleep when possible. Intense evening sessions disturb sleep (E4, 01:29:20).
- **Sets per exercise:** when the coach drafts a week, one exercise gets at most 4 hard sets in a session. Extra sets for a muscle go to another exercise or another day; when no exercise can take them, the draft names the muscle that falls short. The athlete can choose more [NA-DES-009]. This is a pack policy based on 3 to 5 work sets per exercise (see [NA-STR-003]). Conditioning, recovery and cluster work is not limited.
- **Cluster sets:** a cluster is a set split into short mini-sets with a few seconds of rest between them. It counts as its total reps divided by the rep max its load matches: 6 mini-sets of 6 reps at a 12-rep-max load count as 3 hard sets [NA-DES-010]. This is an estimate with no standard source. A cluster keeps its prescription from week to week; the straight exercises take the rest of the muscle's sets.
- **Blocks:** write blocks of about 6 to 12 weeks, with every session planned and a rough idea of where the block sits in the year (E2, 01:21:00).

## The weekly frames

The templates turn this into three weekly frames.

- **Three days** [NA-DES-006]: day 1 speed and power, then hypertrophy; day 2 strength, then high-heart-rate conditioning; day 3 long-duration endurance. Off days are fully off or easy movement. Two sessions already touch most adaptations.
- **Four days** [NA-DES-007]: day 1 whole-body lifting at about 5 to 10 reps; day 2 long-duration work, which doubles as an easy day after the lifting; day 3 muscular endurance at 11 to 30 reps (circuits, body-weight work or a class also fit); day 4 moderate intervals, finished with a few minutes near maximal heart rate.
- **Five days** [NA-DES-008]: the three-day rotation with fewer rest days. Lifting splits into a lower-body and an upper-body emphasis, so each muscle still gets about 48 hours between hard sessions. The source suggests running the three-day rotation back to back; the exact five-day layout is this pack's choice.

Each template has an `emphasis` table. It scales the sets per adaptation for five goals: all-around, strength, hypertrophy, endurance and general health. The frame stays the same; only the amount of each kind of work changes. Galpin makes the same point: to gain more of one quality, do more of that work inside the same frame.

## The year

A simple year has four quarters, each with its own primary goal, and ends each quarter with a week off or a deload (see [NA-PROG-007]). The sample in the source (muscle, then leanness, then conditioning, then long aerobic work) is only an example. Choose the order from the athlete's goals and seasons.
