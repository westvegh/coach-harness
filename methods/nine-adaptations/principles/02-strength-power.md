# 2. Strength and power

## The 3-to-5 frame

Speed, power and strength share one simple structure. Dr. Galpin presents it as an easy way to remember the ranges, not as his invention. Each variable sits between 3 and 5:

| Variable | Range | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Training days per week | 3-5 | [NA-STR-003] |
| Exercises per session | 3-5 | [NA-STR-003] |
| Work sets per exercise | 3-5 | [NA-STR-003] |
| Reps per work set | 3-5 (1-2 is also fine) | [NA-STR-001] |
| Rest between sets | 3-5 minutes | [NA-STR-002] |

Warm-up sets do not count toward the work sets. The bottom of every range still produces results. The top costs more time and recovery. On a low-energy day, the bottom of the range is a complete session, not a failure.

The engine audits strength slots against the rep range [NA-STR-001] and the rest range [NA-STR-002]. Sets of 6 or more reps with short rest drift toward hypertrophy, which is a different adaptation. When the athlete is too tired to keep heavy sets in this range, the right move is a shorter session, not lighter weights for more reps.

## What separates strength from power: the load

The structure stays the same, but the load changes the adaptation.

- **Strength:** work sets at about 70% of one-rep max or heavier. Below that, a set trains something other than maximal strength (E2, 01:55:47).
- **Power:** roughly 30-70% of one-rep max, 5 reps or fewer, and the athlete tries to move every rep as fast as possible [NA-STR-004]. Intent is the whole point. A rep done at moderate speed does not train power, however light the load is.

Power and speed sets get the same 3-5 minutes of rest as strength sets [NA-STR-002]. They must stay fast, so they must never turn into conditioning. A dedicated speed session can be as short as 20 minutes.

Typical power tools: jumps, medicine-ball throws, short sprints, Olympic-lift variations, kettlebell swings and loaded jumps.

## Warming up

Before strength and power work, warm up with a few minutes of dynamic movement in several directions. Then ramp up on the first lift until the athlete feels sharp and fast. That can take more sets than people expect, and the time is well spent. Before hypertrophy work, a short warm-up is enough: the first work set can double as the last warm-up [NA-STR-005].

## Practical notes for the coach

- Put the most important lift first, while the athlete is fresh (see [NA-ADP-003]).
- A set is a work set only when the load and the intent match the target. A light, slow set in a strength slot is a warm-up.
- Supersets of non-competing lifts (for example a press paired with a row) save a lot of time and cost a little peak strength. Use them when time is short. Avoid them in the last weeks before a strength test.
- Heavy lower-body lifts can take larger absolute jumps than light upper-body lifts (see [NA-PROG-004]).
