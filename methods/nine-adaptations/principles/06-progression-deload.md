# 6. Progression and deload

Without progressive overload nothing improves. With too much of it, recovery falls behind. This section holds the pack's only hard training limits.

## How fast to progress

- **Hard cap:** weekly volume for a muscle never rises more than 10% over the reference week [NA-PROG-001]. The engine's progression tools apply it, and the audit checks it. The reference week is the last completed build week that was not a deload or a test week.
- **Small bases:** when a muscle's reference week has fewer than 10 sets, one extra set a week is always allowed, because a 10% step on a small base is less than one set [NA-PROG-009]. This is a harness policy.
- **Target pace:** aim for about 5-7% more volume a week, or about 3% more intensity [NA-PROG-002]. Progress one or two variables at a time, not everything at once. Overload can come from any variable: load, sets, reps, exercises, tempo, frequency, shorter rest or harder movements.
- **Watch the total, not the parts.** Adding "just one mile" to each of three runs turns a 10-mile week into a 13-mile week, a 30% jump. The cap applies to the weekly total for each muscle.

A worked example: raising a muscle from 64 to 72 weekly sets is +12.5%, so the audit reports it and the coach plans fewer sets. The engine's tests keep this case.

## Top sets

A planned top set may not exceed the last logged top set of the same exercise by more than 10% [NA-PROG-003]. This is a pack policy. The source applies its 10% weekly ceiling to lifting in general, and this pack applies it to each exercise's top set. It is a safety ceiling, not a target. The usual target is closer to 3% a week (see [NA-PROG-002]).

Absolute jumps depend on the lift. A heavy squat or deadlift can usually take about 5 lb a week, because 5 lb is a small share of the load. A light upper-body lift often needs a smaller step, or a rep added instead of load [NA-PROG-004].

Known gap: for very light loads, the smallest available step (for example a 20 lb to 25 lb dumbbell) can exceed 10%. The audit reports that step. Progress with reps instead, until the engine supports a minimum increment.

## Deloads

- **Cycle:** deload every 6th week: 5 build weeks, then 1 deload week [NA-PROG-005]. The source also accepts 4 to 8 weeks between deloads; 6 is this pack's default.
- **Depth:** a deload week drops both volume and load to about 70% of the last build week [NA-PROG-006]. For example, 3 sets of 15 might become 2 sets of 8 with a slightly lighter load.
- **Plan them in advance.** Put deloads in the calendar when the block is written, not when the athlete already feels run down. A deload taken only on demand always comes late. End each quarter with a deload or a full week off [NA-PROG-007].
- **Coming back:** the first build week after a deload may return to the best pre-deload week, plus at most one regular step [NA-PROG-008]. The engine enforces this (contracts §8).

## Why these numbers

Each training stress needs time to be absorbed before the gain shows. Small weekly steps keep the new stress in step with recovery. A planned drop every few weeks clears the accumulated fatigue, so the fitness built during the block becomes visible. Followed for years, this rhythm lets an athlete keep progressing without burnout or overuse injuries.
