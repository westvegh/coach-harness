# 7. Recovery and readiness

## Three kinds of evidence

To decide whether an athlete is overreaching, Dr. Galpin looks at three independent kinds of evidence (E5, 01:27:31):

1. **Performance:** the athlete's output drops. Examples: missed reps, a lower top set, slower times, a lower jump.
2. **Physiology:** a marker the athlete cannot influence by effort moves the wrong way. Examples: lower HRV, a higher resting heart rate.
3. **Symptoms:** how the athlete feels. Examples: poor sleep, low energy, unusual soreness, stress, low drive.

Each kind has weaknesses on its own. A low jump can mean the athlete did not try. A wearable score can be noise. So no single measure should drive a decision. The harness maps the three kinds onto its three readiness categories: performance, wearable and check-in.

## Daily decisions: patterns, not scores

- **One morning is not a pattern.** A flag on a single day, in any category, earns a note and no change [NA-REC-008]. HRV often dips the morning after a hard session; that is the training stress doing its job. The pack looks at the trend, not the daily score.
- **Three mornings in a row is a short pattern.** When HRV stays below, or resting heart rate stays above, the athlete's own band on 3 or more mornings in a row, reassess the load [NA-REC-009]. The harness holds load progression and lightens the session. It also asks about sleep, eating and drinking, without setting any targets. A morning with no reading is unknown: it is not counted as off, and it ends the run.
- **The trim.** On the short pattern, the session keeps its exercises and loads and loses about 20% of its hard sets. If the check-in or performance is also red that day, the day becomes a recovery day of easy aerobic work, mobility and breathing [NA-REC-010]. The size and the escalation are this pack's choices, not the source's. Only one adjustment a day. Precedence: the urgent stop, then a recovery day, then a trim, then normal progression.
- **Not wanting to train is a signal.** Not wanting to train at all (aversion, not ordinary tiredness) is a reason to rest. The check-in asks "How much do you want to train today?" from 1 to 5. A 1 offers a recovery day; an unattended run makes the day a recovery day, because the safer change is always allowed [NA-REC-012].
- **Low HRV plus short sleep is one category.** Both come from the wearable, so they count as one flag, not two.

## Program decisions

Change the program, not just a session, only when performance, physiology and symptoms all point the same way on at least 3 separate days [NA-REC-003]. A long pattern is the trigger the harness watches: off-mornings 7 or more days in a row, with a low check-in and a performance drop on the same day. Then it proposes an early deload or a new block, and the athlete approves it [NA-REC-011].

Duration matters as much as direction. A dip of a day or two is normal in hard training and is often the planned stress. More than about 5 days in a row of declining performance deserves action. Close to a competition, act sooner [NA-REC-004].

## Overreaching and overtraining

Recovery runs on four levels: normal fatigue from one session (hours to days), short-term overreaching (days to a week, after which fitness rises above where it started), non-functional overreaching (weeks to recover, with no gain), and true overtraining (months to return to baseline). Short-term overreaching is part of a good program. Non-functional overreaching is common and needs a deload. True overtraining is rare, and it can only be confirmed afterwards, by how long recovery takes (E5, 00:44:06).

## Reading HRV

Compare HRV only with the athlete's own baseline, never with other people. Devices measure and compute it in different ways. Build the baseline from about a month or more of data (the harness waits for 28 valid days within 6 weeks), then compare today with the recent weekly average and the longer history. Compare like with like: same device, same time of day, same conditions [NA-REC-005]. The harness never mixes HRV methods (RMSSD and SDNN) in one baseline.

## Recovery tools

- **Easy movement:** light, low-effort contractions (walking, easy cycling, easy swimming) pump fluid out of sore tissue and ease soreness. Keep it genuinely easy [NA-REC-006].
- **Breathing:** a few minutes of slow nasal breathing after training, such as box breathing (equal counts in, hold, out, hold) or long-exhale sighs, shifts the body toward recovery.
- **Sleep** is the base of recovery. A run of short nights counts more than any single bad night.
- Use one physical tool and one breathing tool per session. Stacking many tools adds little.

## Pain is not soreness

Soreness is expected after new or hard work. Pain is different. A painful region is loaded only as the athlete chose [NA-REC-007]:

- **Avoid it for now:** no exercise that loads the region (a ban in the limits file).
- **Go light and build back slowly:** a cap on sets or load that rises a step at a time while the region feels fine (a cap with `build_back`).
- **It's fine now:** train normally; the coach notes it.

The coach offers the choice kindly, puts the caution in the option text, and does not refuse to plan. It says once: see a clinician for new pain, pain that gets worse, or pain that does not settle. Urgent warning signs (chest pain, fainting, new numbness or weakness) are different: they stop all training changes until a clinician has seen the athlete or it was a false alarm.
